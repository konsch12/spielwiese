Session 1

Introduction

Demographic Transition

  • Rapid population growth is temporary

  • happened at different times, but all countries have begun demographic transition

  • Triggers:

    • industrialization/economic growth

    • improvement in health conditions

    • female empowerment and employment

  • Role of education: better health prevention , technological progress -> education, child quality -> lower fertility

Fertility Transition

  • ready and able to reduce fertility

    • low mortality -> enough children survive

    • economic status quo if not having children

    • acceptable means of contraception

    • quantity-quality trade-off

      • upwards social mobility

      • increased returns to education

  • importance of women’s situation

    • gender roles in society

    • economic and political empowerment of women

  • social and political situation

    • human capital growth (education number and quality of people)

    • institutions and democracy

Transition from natural to controlled fertility

  • marriage: from early and universal to later or maybe never

  • birth spacing: within marriage, longer breastfeeding, contraception

  • Contraception: abortion, coitus interruptus, pill (1960s), sterilization

  • Due to improved health -> better reproductive capacity

    • fewer stillbirths/miscarriages

    • decline in widowhood

  • => fertility rebound just after mortality decrease, before fertility decrease

Session 2

Fertility transition across world regions

Definitions

period life expectancy = average #years a newborn would live if age-specific mortality rates in the current year were to stay the same throughout its life

Period fertility measures

Crude birth rate: number of births per thousand persons in a given population within a given year

\[CBR = \frac{\text{births in year y}}{\text{population in year y}} * 1000 = \frac{B}{P} * 1000\]

General fertility rate: number of births per thousand women of ‘reproductive age’ in a given population, within a given year

\[ GFR = \frac{\text{births in year y}}{\text{women age 15-49 in year y}} * 1000 = \frac{B}{\sum_{x = 15}^{49}{W_x}} * 1000\]

Age-specific fertility rates : number of births per thousand women of a given age \(x\), in a given population in a given year

\[ ASFR_x = \frac{\text{births at age x}}{\text{women of age x at risk}} * 1000 \]

Using age groups of size \(n\), over a period \(p\)

\[ASFR_{[x, x+n], p} = \frac{B_{[x,x+n], p}}{W_{[x,x+n], p}}* 1000\]

Total fertility rate : fertility level standardized per age, number of children per woman, births occurring in the course of one year, standardized for age. Corresponds to one woman who has the average number of children of each age across all her reproductive life.

TFR = total number of children of women born in a fictive birth cohort, were they behaving at each age like in the current year.

Single Ages: \[TFR = \sum_{x = 15}^{49}{\frac{ASFR_x}{1000}} = \sum_x{\frac{B_x}{W_x}}\] Age Groups of size \(n\): \[TFR = \sum_{x = 15, 20, ...}^{49}{\frac{ASFR_{[x, x + n]}*n}{1000}}\]

Synthetic cohort

for year y, a hypothetical cohort who would behave at each sage the same way as in year y.

age structure is fixed, assumed no mortality and no migration between ages 15-49. -> often not the case

Assumptions:

  • independence: mortality and migration independent of past fertility

  • continuity: everyone behaves in the same way

  • current conditions: fertility only depends on current conditions, not paste

Limitations of TFR:

  • sensitive to short term variation

  • TFR can be equal between countries, despite different ASFR

  • TFR reflects changes in the timing of birth -> lower at time of postponement

Advantage of TFR: standardized measure, comparable over time and across countries

De Brujin: Theory or Framework?

Demography:

  • adequate descriptive elements

  • no grand unifying theory

  • interdisciplinary sub-narratives

  • independent inputs contribute to overall explanation

Fertility transition:

  • mortality decline, modernization

  • collapse of normative and ideational systems that support fertility

  • intermediate determinants

  • diffusion of ideas of fertiltiy and contraception

Context and individual processes necessary

Fertility decline approaches:

Social Organization approach

  • Ideational change -> attitudes, values, importance of fertility ideals and intentions, consciousness

  • Innovation and diffusion -> between groups, acceptance of new ideas and technologies (contraception)

Culture Approach

  • shared values and beliefs, intergenerational transmission

  • ethnicity, language, region, religion, …

Institutional approach

  • kinship, family systems, socioal systems

  • Fertility: situational specific and dependent on histories

Economic approach

  • Quantity-Quality trade-off: children better educated -> more value, less children; children loose economic values, cost increases

  • Opporuntity cost of children: loss of income (higher wages), not an insurance against widowhood

  • Wealth flow theory

    • parents make rational decisions based on direction of intergenerational transfers

    • parents no wealth -> children need to provide net wealth

    • parents wealthy -> less children

Limitations:

  • variations in patterns of fertility decline

  • wide range of historical contexts

  • Eurocentrism

Specific knowledge needed:

  • socio-economic and cultural settings

  • mechanisms of social change

  • choices of individuals within a context (structure-agency interaction)

Criticism of SDT

Theory of Gender revolution

  • instead of declining -> increasing fertility rates

  • in SDT: not much space for place of women in society

  • more importance to change in gender relations

  • more children when men/women are equal -> men contributing to household, women able to rely on men

  • 2 stage process:

    • entry of women in labour market -> fertility decline

    • increased involvement of men in household -> re-increase of fertility

Developmental Paradigm

  • reliance on cross-sectional data

  • forcing multidimensional observations into linear narrative -> reading data from afterwards perspective -> bias

  • weak empirical underpinnings (counter-evidence)

  • developmental approach -> expects all countries follow same path (as Europe)

  • important regional variation

  • ethnocentric bias, descriptions based on Western societies

New Home Economics

  • interest marriage is explained by sexual division of task and complementarity between spouses

  • entry of women into job market -> increase in divorces

  • pessimistic vision on the future of marriage

Globalization Theory

  • structural changes worldwide due to globalization

  • high level of structural uncertainty

  • job market uncertainty -> young adults forgo long-term binding commitments (marriage, parenthood) -> less trustful

Pattern of Disadvantage

  • increase in non-marital births

  • According to SDT: happening among highly-educated people first, then spread

  • PoD: happening among poorest people, observed in Russia, US

    • people feel that they do not have the means to marry -> have children unmarried

    • unmarried cohabitation out of economic necessity

    • weakened by not getting married -> not protected

    • having children is important aspect of integration/recognition in society, 2000s

Educational Patterns in Cohabitation

  • Europe: clear upatake among highly educated -> SDT

  • North America: higher levels in disadvantaged strata -> Pattern of Disadvantage

  • Latin America: diverse

  • Asia: very rare, Japan after crisis: Pattern of disadvantage, China: SDT

  • Africa: rise, SDT or Pattern of disadvantage

Change in Expectations

  • Deinstitutionalization of marriage
    • marriage has weakened in Europe and US
    • alternative acceptable and more prevalent
    • changes in roles
  • Gender egalitarisnism
    • gaining more dominant normative status
    • women work -> men in household -> still inequalities
    • more union stabilty
